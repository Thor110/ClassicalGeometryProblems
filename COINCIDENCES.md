# Coincidences

Loose notes on the patterns that turn up while working on the archive: some of them meaningful, most of them
not, and all of them fun. Nothing here is a claim about the problems themselves.

## Thirteen point four gold atoms

In November 2026 the [Architectural Vision](pages/architecture.html) page worked out that the best oversized and
undersized squarings of the circle on the charts, built on a circle 100 m across, end **3.86 nanometres** apart:
about **13.4 gold atoms** side by side.

Thirteen is Edward's lucky number, chosen because people called it unlucky and that seemed unfair to the number.
And 1 + 3 = 4, and 4.13 read backwards is 3.14.

It is worth being honest about how much of that is the number and how much is us:

- **The size is a choice.** The page measures a circle 100 m across because 100 is a round number. On a circle
  200 m across the same gap is 26.8 gold atoms; on one 37.3 m across it is 5.0.
- **The atom is a choice.** Gold was picked because of the gold seam in Vision II, and its atom is taken as
  0.288 nm across (twice its metallic radius). Measured in hydrogen atoms, or in silicon, the count is different.
- **The gap will move.** It is set by the two best entries on the squaring chart. A better construction on either
  side shrinks it, and the page works it out live, so the 13.4 lasts only as long as both records stand.

So 13.4 is a true measurement of one particular pair, at one particular size, in one particular unit, today. That
it lands on a number with a story is a coincidence, and a good one.

## Why we see patterns everywhere

People are very good at finding patterns, so good that we find them where nothing put them: faces in clouds,
messages in number plates, a lucky number in a gap between two lines. The name for it is *apophenia*. It helped our
ancestors spot the lion in the grass, and it costs us little to see a few lions that are not there.

There is also simple arithmetic behind it. Work with enough numbers, as the archive does with every entry, every
gap and every scale, and some of them will land near a number that means something to you. With thousands of
chances, a match is not surprising; it would be strange to find none. (At the time of writing, 13 people are listed
under "Also found by" across the charts. That one is a coincidence too.)

None of this makes the patterns less enjoyable. It just means that a pattern is the start of a question, not
the answer to one.

## Patterns that are real: geometry follows from simple shapes

Some repeats in the archive are not coincidences at all. A straightedge and compass can only make a few kinds of
length in a few steps: the square's diagonal √2, the equilateral triangle's height √3, the √5 of the double square,
and the golden ratio that comes out of it. Anyone who starts from a circle and a square is choosing from the same
short list, so the same values keep being found again, centuries apart and by people who never read each other:

- **Ten values on the charts are reached by more than one construction.** Cusa's fifth squaring (about 1450),
  Crelle's (1846) and Lemoine's (1895) all come to π = √(8(√5 − 1)), by different steps.
- **The same construction is found again.** Molther (1619), Huygens (1654) and Busch (1846) each laid three
  diameters and a fifth of the quadrant's chord along a line. The archive lists 13 such later finders under
  "Also found by".
- **The same numbers return.** Stifel's 5/4 for the doubled cube (1544) is also where Winthrop's construction of
  1793 ends up as its angles shrink. Finé's 3 − √2 (1556) for ∛4 is the square of Sorgner's √(3 − √2) for ∛2
  (1915).

These come from the shapes, not from luck: the circle, the square and the triangle hold only so many simple
lengths, and good approximations are made from simple lengths. That is part of why trying is worthwhile. Each
attempt walks the same small landscape, and sometimes finds a path nobody else noticed.
