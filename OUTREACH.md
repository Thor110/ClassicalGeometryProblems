# Outreach: drafts and places to send them

For Edward to adapt and send himself, one message at a time, through each organisation's own
published contact route. Nothing here should be sent in bulk.

A few habits that help these get read:

- Address a person or a named role where the website gives one ("Dear Editor", "Dear Communications Officer").
- Change the first line for each recipient, so it is clearly written to them.
- Keep it short and link to the site. The site does the explaining.
- Send from your own address, with your full name, and reply to anyone who answers.
- Do not follow up more than once.

---

## 1. Email to a mathematical society, journal or department

**Subject:** An open archive for circle-squaring and trisection submissions

> Dear [name or role],
>
> I am writing because [the Society / your journal / your department], like many in mathematics,
> probably receives letters claiming to have squared the circle, trisected the angle or doubled the
> cube with straightedge and compass.
>
> I have built an open archive that may take some of that weight off you. It accepts these
> constructions as what they are, approximations, and measures, ranks and credits them by public
> rules. The site explains why exact solutions are impossible (Wantzel, 1837; Lindemann, 1882),
> while giving the sender's work a fair hearing and a place on the charts with their name on it, as
> the tradition from Kochański to Ramanujan deserves.
>
> The charts hold well over a hundred constructions across five problems, among them historical
> ones rebuilt and checked against their original sources, from Dürer to Ramanujan. Every GeoGebra
> file is rebuilt and measured automatically, the counting rules are published, and new submissions
> are checked by an automated workflow before a person reviews them. People's work and AI search finds are kept apart.
>
> For anyone who receives these claims, there is a short referral message on the site that can be
> sent back as a reply, so that a claim can be answered in a minute without being checked by hand:
> https://thor110.github.io/ClassicalGeometryProblems/pages/why-try.html#referral
>
> If you think it would be useful, I would be grateful if you could pass it to whoever handles such
> letters, or mention it in [your newsletter / your news section].
>
> Website: https://thor110.github.io/ClassicalGeometryProblems/
> Repository: https://github.com/Thor110/ClassicalGeometryProblems
>
> With thanks,
> Edward James Gordon

## 2. Announcement post

### Long version (a blog, Hacker News, a forum)

**Title:** An open archive for approximations to the impossible classical geometry problems

> Squaring the circle, trisecting an angle and doubling the cube were proved impossible with
> straightedge and compass in the 1800s. People still try, and mathematicians still receive their
> letters. Most get deleted, and the senders get a curt reply, or none.
>
> I have been building approximations to these problems for years, and I built an open archive for
> them. It treats them as what they are: approximations, some remarkably close. Kochański's 1685
> construction of the circumference is good to 99.998%; Ramanujan's from 1914 to 99.99999%.
>
> What it does:
>
> - Charts every construction by accuracy and by how many circles and lines it takes, oversized and
>   undersized separately, with the name of whoever found it.
> - Rebuilds every GeoGebra file from its starting points and measures it to 32 digits, so no figure
>   on the charts is taken on trust. Counting rules, micro-scale adjustments and shallow crossings are
>   all published and checked.
> - Accepts submissions straight from GeoGebra through a small browser extension. Each one is
>   evaluated automatically: it checks the construction really answers the problem chosen, measures
>   it, and compares it with everything already charted.
> - Gives mathematicians and journals a ready reply for the claims they receive, pointing senders to
>   a place where their work will be measured fairly, and where they can learn why exactness is
>   impossible.
>
> There is also a side project: what it would take to build these constructions as architecture,
> with the gap between the best oversized and undersized lines as a path you can walk, lit by the
> sun at the solstice. (The best ones would need a plaza most of the way across the Earth. The
> buildable ones fit in a park.)
>
> An AI assistant helped build the website and tools, and ran searches of its own; its finds are
> charted separately from people's work.
>
> https://thor110.github.io/ClassicalGeometryProblems/
> https://github.com/Thor110/ClassicalGeometryProblems

For **Hacker News**, post as "Show HN: An open archive for approximations to the impossible
classical geometry problems", with the link to the site, and the text above as your first comment.

## 3. Where to send them

Use each organisation's own contact page or published address, and check it is current before
sending.

### Mathematical societies (email 1)

| Organisation | Why |
|---|---|
| London Mathematical Society (UK) | The UK's main research society; its newsletter reaches departments |
| Institute of Mathematics and its Applications (UK) | Publishes *Mathematics Today* for members across the UK |
| The Mathematical Association (UK) | Publishes *The Mathematical Gazette*; teachers meet these attempts from pupils |
| British Society for the History of Mathematics (UK) | The historical approximations (Kochański, Ramanujan); use the history sentence |
| American Mathematical Society (US) | Publishes the *Notices*; reaches departments across North America |
| Mathematical Association of America (US) | Undergraduate teaching and outreach; its members include those who answer such letters |
| European Mathematical Society | The *EMS Magazine* reaches national societies across Europe |
| Polish Mathematical Society | Kochański was Polish; his construction is on the charts |
| Indian Mathematical Society; Ramanujan Mathematical Society (India) | Ramanujan's two constructions are on the charts |
| Australian, Canadian, New Zealand and South African mathematical societies | The same reasons, in each country |
| International Commission on the History of Mathematics | The historical record, internationally |

### Journals and magazines (the editor's version)

| Publication | Why |
|---|---|
| *The Mathematical Intelligencer* | Has a long interest in mathematical history and in amateur mathematics |
| *The American Mathematical Monthly* and *Mathematics Magazine* (MAA) | Widely read; receive submissions from the public |
| *The Mathematical Gazette* (Mathematical Association) | Read by teachers |
| *Notices of the AMS* | Reaches the whole US community |
| arXiv | Its moderators handle many such papers; worth a single note through its contact route |

Useful background when writing to these: Underwood Dudley's books *A Budget of Trisections* (1987)
and *Mathematical Cranks* (1992) describe how many of these letters mathematicians have always
received. Cite them for the scale of the problem, not the word "crank": the archive's whole point is
to treat the senders kindly.

### Communities and blogs (the announcement post)

| Place | How |
|---|---|
| The Aperiodical | A UK maths news blog; it takes news from readers through its site |
| Mathstodon (mathstodon.xyz) | The maths community on Mastodon; the short version, with #math #geometry |
| Hacker News | "Show HN" post; suits an open project with a working site |
| Reddit: r/mathematics, r/geometry, r/math | Read each subreddit's rules on self-promotion first; r/math is strict, and may want it in a weekly thread |
| GeoGebra community | Relevant because every construction is a GeoGebra file and the extension submits from GeoGebra |
| Math Stack Exchange | Not for posting the announcement; only link the archive in an answer where a question is about approximating the circle or trisection, and say it is yours |

### People who explain maths to the public (a short personal note)

Through their published business or contact addresses: Matt Parker (Stand-up Maths, who has long
celebrated approximations of π), Numberphile, Mathologer and 3Blue1Brown. A short, friendly note
with the link and one striking fact (Ramanujan's 99.99999%, or the plaza the size of the Earth) is
more likely to be read than a long one.

### Teachers (optional)

The Association of Teachers of Mathematics (UK) and the National Council of Teachers of
Mathematics (US): pupils often try trisection, and the archive turns that into a measurable project
rather than a dead end.

## A suggested order

1. Post the long version on The Aperiodical and as a Show HN, and the short version on Mathstodon.
   These cost nothing and show whether the message lands.
2. Send the society email to the LMS, AMS, MAA, EMS and the history societies.
3. Send the editor's version to a few journals.
4. Write the personal notes to the public communicators once the site has had a little attention.
