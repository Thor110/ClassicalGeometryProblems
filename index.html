Classical Geometry Problems // Open Archive

A Catalogue of Straightedge and Compass Approximations, Ranked, Open and Reproducible.
Approximations, Not Claims
Every construction here is an approximation, and is labelled as one, with its accuracy measured and its file open to check. Nothing here claims to overturn the proofs that exact solutions are impossible.
Project Purpose
For over two thousand years people have tried to square the circle, trisect an angle and double the cube with nothing but a straightedge and a compass. We now know exact solutions are impossible. The search for close ones never stopped being worthwhile: it is how Hippocrates found his lunes, how Archimedes bounded π, and how Kochański and Ramanujan left constructions we still draw today.
This archive collects those approximations in one place, ranks them on open charts by accuracy and by how many circles and lines they take, and keeps every GeoGebra file so anyone can check, improve or beat them. Every entry carries a public verification record, with people and AI checks kept apart, and anyone can dispute a figure. It also gives journals, mathematicians and forums one place to point people to, kindly, when someone shares a construction.
Trying Is Not Foolish
Searching for these constructions is fun, and it teaches more geometry than most textbooks. Yet people who share an honest, clearly labelled approximation are too often met with ridicule. Nobody would go back in time and call Archimedes, Kochański or Ramanujan fools for trying. This archive takes the other view: measure the attempt, record it, and credit the person who made it.

Notable Authors
The charts credit more than sixty people, from the scribe of the Rhind papyrus to today. Most are remembered for these constructions alone; some are among the great names of mathematics, who worked on the same problems:
  • Albrecht Dürer (1471–1528): Painter and printmaker; his Underweysung der Messung (1525) brought geometry to artists in German, and gave a squaring offered honestly as a working rule.
  • François Viète (1540–1603): Father of modern algebraic notation and author of the first infinite product for π (1593); he drew the circle's quadrant from the golden section.
  • Christiaan Huygens (1629–1695): Pendulum clock, wave theory of light, Saturn's rings; in 1654 he proved the bounds on π strictly and gave rectifications with proven limits of error.
  • Adam Adamandy Kochański (1631–1700): Court mathematician to John III Sobieski; his 1685 rectification gives π to four places in a few strokes and is still drawn today.
  • Johann Heinrich Lambert (1728–1777): Proved π irrational in 1761, the first step toward showing the circle cannot be squared; he also sharpened Snellius' rectification of arcs.
  • Srinivasa Ramanujan (1887–1920): Infinite series, partitions and modular forms; his series for 1/π drive modern computations, and his squarings give π to six and eight places.
