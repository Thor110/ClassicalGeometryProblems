Classical Geometry Problems // Why Try The Impossible

On the people who searched, why the search is worth it, how to meet someone who shares an attempt, and where to refer claims of a solution.
"I figured even if I couldn't find a solution, that at the least I would have some fun drawing geometric patterns and maybe I could turn a few of them into interesting logo designs."
Edward James Gordon, The Three Classic Geometry Problems
Anyone who looks up these problems meets a wall of statements that they are impossible, and too often an implication that anyone who tries is a fool. Both halves deserve an answer. Exact solutions with straightedge and compass are impossible, and that is proven. Trying is not foolish, and history shows it.
The Searchers
  • c. 440 BCEHippocrates of Chios, trying to square the circle, found the first exact quadrature of a curved figure: his lunes.
  • c. 420 BCEHippias of Elis invented the quadratrix, one of the first curves defined by motion, to trisect the angle.
  • c. 350 BCEMenaechmus, chasing the doubled cube, studied the curves we now call the conic sections. Planetary orbits are ellipses among them.
  • c. 250 BCEArchimedes approached the circle with polygons of 96 sides and proved 3 10⁄71 < π < 3 1⁄7, the approach that dominated the calculation of π for nearly two thousand years.
  • 1685Adam Adamandy Kochański published a straightedge and compass construction of π accurate to about one part in 50,000. It is still drawn in classrooms.
  • 1796Carl Friedrich Gauss, aged 18, constructed the regular 17-sided polygon, the first new constructible regular polygon in two thousand years. Not everything with straightedge and compass had been found.
  • 1837Pierre Wantzel proved that trisecting every angle and doubling the cube are impossible with straightedge and compass.
  • 1882Ferdinand von Lindemann proved π transcendental, so squaring the circle is impossible too.
  • 1913Srinivasa Ramanujan, thirty-one years after Lindemann, published a construction for squaring the circle accurate to about one part in ten million. Nobody calls it foolish. It tops this archive's chart.
  • TodayYou. GeoGebra measures to fifteen decimal places, and anyone with a browser can search. Several methods in these charts were found in the last two years.
An Approximation Is Not A Claim
There is a difference between "I have squared the circle exactly" and "here is a construction that lands within 0.07%, and here is the file". The first contradicts a proof. The second is ordinary, honest geometry, the same kind Kochański and Ramanujan did, and it deserves the same welcome.
Every entry in this archive is the second kind. Each is labelled as an approximation, measured, ranked and open to check. That is the point of the charts: they replace an argument about whether someone is a crank with a number anyone can verify.
Why The Search Is Worth It
  • It teaches geometry. Bisectors, tangents, similar triangles, constructible numbers: you learn them because you need them, not because a syllabus says so.
  • It is a real puzzle. Accuracy against circles and lines is a genuine trade-off. A method at 99.99% with 6 steps can be worth more than one at 99.999% with 60.
  • It is beautiful. The patterns drawn along the way are worth keeping for their own sake.
  • It connects you to history. You are drawing the same figures as Hippocrates, Archimedes and Ramanujan.
  • Understanding why it is impossible is its own reward. Many people first meet field theory and transcendental numbers by asking "why can't I get closer?"
Responding To Someone Who Shares An Attempt
If you are a mathematician, an editor, a moderator or just a reader of a forum, these take less time than a put-down:
  • Read what they actually claim. If they say approximation, treat it as one.
  • Ask for the number. "What accuracy did you measure, and with how many steps?" turns an argument into a comparison.
  • If they do claim an exact solution, say kindly why it cannot be (Wantzel 1837, Lindemann 1882), then point out that their construction is still an approximation worth measuring, and that it has a home here.
  • Credit the effort. Somebody spent hours with a compass. That is more geometry than most people do in a lifetime.
  • Do not pile on. One clear, kind correction helps. Twenty mocking replies teach nothing except that curiosity is punished.
  • Never make it about intelligence. Some of the greatest mathematicians in history worked on exactly these problems.

For Mathematicians, Journals And Moderators
Mathematics departments and journals receive a steady stream of letters claiming to have squared the circle, trisected the angle or doubled the cube. Each one costs someone time, and a curt rejection rarely helps the sender. This archive exists partly to lift that burden: you can refer such claims here instead.
  • The sender gets a fair hearing: their construction is measured by public rules and, if it reaches 95%, charted with their name on it.
  • They learn why exactness is impossible, from the same page that credits their work.
  • You can answer in one short message, without needing to check the construction yourself.

If You Have Been Mocked
You are in good company, and your work has a home here. Measure it, write it up, and submit it. Whether it lands at 95% or 99.999%, it will be recorded with your name on it, judged by its number and nothing else.
The Archive's Promise
  • Every submission is measured the same way, by the same public rules.
  • Credit goes to the person who found a construction first. Contested marks exist to find that person, not to accuse anyone.
  • Corrections are made in the open, on the numbers, without ridicule.
  • Nothing here will be presented as an exact solution to a problem proven impossible.
