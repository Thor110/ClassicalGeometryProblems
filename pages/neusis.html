Classical Geometry Problems // Neusis By Circles

Why the sliding ruler of the oldest exact trisection cannot be drawn with straightedge and compass, why the path it slides along can, and how a circle laid along that path comes within seconds of arc.
The Marked Ruler
The Greeks could trisect any angle exactly, if they were allowed one extra move: neusis, "verging". Mark two points on a ruler, a radius apart, and slide the ruler through a fixed point until the two marks land on two given lines or circles. Archimedes' trisection (Book of Lemmas, Proposition 8) works like this:
  • Draw the angle AOB to be trisected, with the circle about O through A and B, and the line through O and A.
  • Lay the ruler through B and slide it until one mark C is on the circle and the other mark D is on the line, with CD equal to the radius.
  • Then the angle at D is exactly a third of AOB. Since DC = CO, the triangle DCO is isosceles, so the angle COD is θ, the angle at D. The angle OCB, outside that triangle, is 2θ, and so is OBC, since OC = OB. The angle BOA, outside the triangle DOB, is then θ + 2θ = 3θ.

Why You Cannot Draw The Ruler
The position where both marks fit is the answer to a cubic equation: finding the angle θ with 3θ equal to the given angle means solving 4x³ − 3x = cos 3θ. A straightedge and compass can only reach lengths built from square roots, one after another, and Wantzel proved in 1837 that the roots of this cubic cannot be reached that way for most angles. So no finite straightedge-and-compass construction can draw the ruler in its final position. The marked ruler is not a convenience: it is exactly the extra power the construction needs.
But You Can Draw Its Path
Watch the second mark as the ruler slides. It traces a curve, shown in green above: the conchoid of the circle from the point B, which, because B is on the circle, is a limaçon of Pascal. Every single point of that curve can be drawn with straightedge and compass:
  • draw any line through B at an angle you can construct (by halving, say);
  • it meets the circle again at C;
  • the circle about C with the radius marks the point of the curve on that line.
So the ruler's path can be plotted at as many points as you like. What cannot be constructed is the one point where the path crosses the line: that crossing is the cubic again. Everything up to the last step is allowed; only the last step is not.
Neusis By Circles
Near the crossing, the path bends gently, very like a circular arc. So replace it there with a circle, and let the circle cross the line instead. A circle through three points is easy to construct, and its crossing with a line is a straightedge-and-compass point. The best approximations in the archive work the same way, with more care about which circle to take:
  • Eugen Kopf (1933): as Finsler showed, his second construction amounts to replacing the limaçon near the answer with a circle chosen to be exact at 90° as well as at 0°, an interpolating circle. It is within 15 seconds of arc for every acute angle.
  • Paul Finsler (1938) explained why Kopf's is so good, and took instead the circle of curvature of the limaçon at the starting point, the circle that hugs the curve most closely there. His error grows only with the fifth power of the angle; with the angle first halved twice, it is 0.016 seconds of arc at 22½°.
Other Ways Around
  • Correct and repeat. Lay the ruler in a guessed position, construct where its mark actually lands, and correct the guess. Each round is an ordinary straightedge-and-compass step, and a few rounds come very close. Pellet's method of 1888 works this way; the archive lists it on the Wanted page, because it gives no fixed number of rounds.
  • Fold the paper. A single fold can make a neusis exactly: Margherita Beloch showed in 1936 that one fold places two points on two lines at once. That is why origami can trisect angles and double cubes, which straightedge and compass cannot.
  • Build the ruler. Sliders, linkages, marked rulers and ropes all do what the rules forbid, and do it exactly. They are kept apart from the charts, under Mechanical Solutions.
