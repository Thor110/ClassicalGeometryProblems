Classical Geometry Problems // The Six Problems

What each problem asks, why no exact construction exists, and what an approximation is aiming for.
The Rules
Every approximation in this archive stays strictly Euclidean: an unmarked straightedge and a compass, and no measuring. You may draw a line through two points you already have, draw a circle about a point through another point, and mark where lines and circles cross. That is all.
Those moves can only ever produce lengths built from whole numbers by adding, subtracting, multiplying, dividing and taking square roots. That single fact is the reason each problem below has no exact answer, and the reason the approximations are interesting at all.
6. π Approximations
Construct a length of π from a length of one. Every method for rectifying the circle gives one: halve the constructed circumference of a circle of radius 1. The halving is a midpoint, which is not counted, so the accuracy and the circles and lines stay the same. The π chart is worked out from the circle rectification chart, entry for entry, with each construction's value of π written out. Kochański's construction of 1685 is still one of the most elegant, and is walked through on the constructions page.

    7. Causal Links
    The six problems are not independent. Some are the same problem in another form, some share their tools, and one pair has no exact link at all, only a near-coincidence. Knowing which is which shows where an approximation to one problem gives an approximation to another, and at what accuracy.
    The circle and π: one problem in four forms.
    • Arc rectification gives rectifying the circle. A circle is four quarter arcs, or two half arcs, so a method that rectifies any arc rectifies the circle, at the accuracy it has for that arc.
    • Rectifying the circle and π are the same. Halving a circumference is a midpoint, and doubling a length is one swing of the compass. The accuracy carries over unchanged.
    • Rectifying the circle and squaring it are the same. From a length of π times the radius, the square's side is the geometric mean of that length and the radius: one semicircle and one perpendicular. The square's area then has exactly the accuracy of the length. Back again, a square's side gives π times the radius by similar triangles.
    Cube roots: two problems that share their tools. Trisecting an angle and doubling the cube both need the root of a cubic, which straightedge and compass cannot reach. Any tool that solves cubics solves both: a marked ruler (the Greeks' neusis), conic sections, Nicomedes' conchoid, or folding paper. Neither gives the other with straightedge and compass, though: trisection solves the cubics with three real roots, doubling the cube one with a single real root (Gleason, 1988).
    The bridge: rectifying arcs both ways. Rectifying an arc straightens it. If you could also do the reverse, bending a straight length back onto the circle as an arc, you could trisect any angle: straighten the angle's arc, divide the straight length into three, which is easy, and bend one third back. The same works for dividing an angle in any ratio. The Greeks found curves that do exactly this: Hippias's quadratrix (about 420 BC) trisects angles and, as Dinostratus showed, squares the circle; Archimedes' spiral does both too. With approximations, the errors of the two directions add.
    No link: squaring the circle and doubling the cube. π is transcendental (Lindemann, 1882) and ∛2 is a root of a cubic. Having one gives no way to the other with straightedge and compass: the cubic for ∛2 stays a cubic however many square roots and multiples of π are added.
    A near-coincidence between the families. Draw a cube's outline as a hexagon of its edges, and the circle around it. Then draw the doubled cube's outline the same way. The doubled cube's outline is that circle plus one more of its own edges: unroll the circle, and it takes up five of the doubled cube's six edges, leaving exactly one over. Each edge is one sixth of the doubled outline and one fifth of the circle, so the doubled edge is close to 2π/5 times the original, against the true ∛2. The two differ by only 0.26%. It is not an exact link, which cannot exist, but it holds at any size, and it gives a way to double the cube through the circle: rectify the circle around the original cube and take a fifth of it, for 99.22% by volume, undersized.
    Noticed by Edward James Gordon in his doubling construction Cube 2 Tesseract v2.0 (charted at 98.924%; titled DTC-99.487 in his GeoGebra account), where the doubled outline measures 43.163 and the circle 36.005, leaving 7.158 against an edge of 7.194. Measured both ways: in his construction, five edges fall short of the circle by just 0.10%, and the part left over is 0.5% short of an edge; with a cube doubled exactly, five edges overshoot the circle by 0.26%, and the part left over is 1.3% longer than an edge. His construction sits closer to the circle than the exact doubling does, because its own small error leans towards the circle: if every link could be resolved exactly, the pattern would hold only to 0.26%. open it in GeoGebra · .ggb.
    Derived Squares
    Each rectification of the circle, carried on to a square by the geometric mean, squares the circle at the same accuracy. The derived square takes about three more circles and three more lines (laying the radius beside the length, the semicircle, the perpendicular, and raising the square). Derived squares are not entries on the squaring chart, which ranks constructions built as squares; they are shown here to show what each rectification is worth as a square.

    Understanding Impossibility
    Straightedge and compass constructions generate only numbers reachable through nested square roots of rational numbers. Such numbers are always roots of polynomials whose degree is a power of two.
    • Squaring and rectifying the circle need π (or √π). Lindemann proved in 1882 that π is transcendental: it is not the root of any polynomial with rational coefficients, so it is out of reach.
    • Doubling the cube needs ∛2, a root of x³ − 2. Trisecting an angle in general needs roots of cubics such as 8x³ − 6x − 1 (for 60°). Neither cubic factors, so their degree is 3, not a power of two. Pierre Wantzel proved both impossible in 1837.
    • Some angles can be trisected exactly (90° can, as 30° is constructible). The impossibility is that no single method works for every angle.
    So every method in this archive is an approximation, with an accuracy that can be measured and compared. Relax the rules (a marked ruler, a rope, a special curve) and exact answers do exist. Those are kept separately under mechanical solutions.
    How Many Approximations Are There?
    Almost certainly infinitely many. With no limit on the number of steps, or on how small a step may be, constructions can be refined forever, creeping ever closer to the true value without reaching it.
    The fair ones are another matter. Cap the number of circles and lines, as the rulesets do, and only so many constructions can be made: from a fixed starting figure, a fixed number of steps can be combined in only a limited number of ways. Leave out micro scale steps as well, ones too small to reproduce on paper, and the field narrows further. So the approximations that need no micro scale adjustments and no more steps than the rules allow are quite possibly a limited and calculable set: one that could, in principle, be counted and searched in full.
    The search finds on the charts sample that set. Searching it completely, for small step counts at least, would show the best approximation that can ever be made within the rules.
    Terminology
    • Rational number: a number expressible as a ratio of two integers, such as 22/7.
    • Irrational number: a number that cannot be written as a ratio of integers, such as √2.
    • Constructible number: a length reachable from a unit length with straightedge and compass. √2 is constructible; ∛2 is not.
    • Transcendental number: a number that is not the root of any non-zero polynomial with rational coefficients, such as π or e.
    • Oversized / undersized: whether a construction lands above or below the true value.
    • Accuracy: 100 − |constructed − true| ÷ true × 100, reported to three decimal places, truncated rather than rounded.
